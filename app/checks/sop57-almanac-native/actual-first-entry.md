---
title: "Why the Horizon Always Sits at Eye Level"
---
Stand on a flat beach and look at the sea. Now crouch. The horizon drops with you. Stand on a lifeguard chair and it rises. The horizon is not a feature of the world so much as a feature of *you*: it lies exactly at the height of your eyes, every time, wherever you are standing on level ground.

The reason is a small piece of projective geometry, the same geometry Renaissance painters rediscovered when they worked out linear perspective. Imagine a transparent picture plane held upright a distance $d$ in front of your eye, and let your eye be a height $h$ above the ground. A point on the ground at horizontal distance $D$ ahead of you is seen along a line that crosses the picture plane at a height

$$y = -\frac{h\,d}{D}$$

below eye level (similar triangles: the ratio of drop to forward distance is the same for the near picture plane as for the far ground point). As $D$ grows, $y$ shrinks toward zero. Infinitely far ground points land exactly at eye level. The horizon is the image of the ground plane's *line at infinity*, and the synthetic statement is cleaner still: the plane through your eye parallel to the ground meets the picture plane in a line, and that line is the horizon. No measurement is needed; it follows from parallelism alone.

Here is where it touches everyday perception. Suppose a person of height $H$ stands on the same flat ground at distance $D$. Their feet project to $-hd/D$ and the top of their head to $(H-h)d/D$. The horizon (height $0$) therefore cuts their image at a fraction

$$\frac{h}{H}$$

of the way up from their feet, and $D$ has cancelled out. If they are your height, the horizon passes through their eyes whether they are three paces away or three hundred. If it passes through their chin, they are taller than you; through their forehead, shorter. A crowd of adults on a beach shows this vividly: all their heads cluster along one line, the far ones tiny and the near ones large, feet fanning downward with distance, eyes pinned to the horizon.

Perception researchers call this the *horizon ratio*, and there is good reason to think people use it without noticing. It explains why a figure painted with its eyes on the horizon reads as an adult of ordinary height, while the same figure with the horizon at its waist reads as a giant or as a viewer who is lying down. Photographers exploit it: lowering the camera to a child's eye level makes adults loom, because the horizon now cuts them at the knees.

The surprising part is that none of this requires knowing any distance. Projective geometry discards lengths entirely and keeps only incidence, which lines meet where. Yet that stripped-down structure is enough to let your eye compare heights across a whole landscape at a glance.
