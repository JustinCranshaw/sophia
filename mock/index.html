Sophia: the experience

Each notebook is an emergent textbook. You guide every step, and the book grows to your needs one accepted page at a time. Beside it runs a conversation with Sophia that does two jobs: it makes the book, and it walks you back through it.

The book

Chapters and pages. Sophia writes and revises them; you can edit any section yourself, and your lines stay yours until you let her change them. Every page keeps its sources, your own words where they were used, and one previous version.
notebook.json · pages/*.md · pages/*.previous.md

The conversation

One running thread per notebook, in dated sessions.
  • Making the book: explaining, challenging, researching, drafting pages and revisions.
  • Guiding through the book: reading a page with you, stopping to ask, answering questions about a passage.

Goals

What you want from the notebook, in your words, and what "there" looks like. Progress is shown as evidence from your attempts and pages, not as a score. Sophia can propose a goal; only you accept one or mark it met.

Sophia's notes

Your attempts in your words, Sophia's tentative read of them, open questions, and the next step. Sophia uses them to brief you when you return. You can see and delete every one, and they never reach a page unless you accept that page.

Decisions

Settled

  • The book and the conversation are separate surfaces. Once a notebook has a page, the book takes the middle of the window (PLAN.md §5).
  • Emergent textbook: Sophia may suggest directions, but drafts nothing until you choose.
  • Drafts and revisions appear in the book where they'd go. You accept the exact text; only Accept saves.
  • One conversation per notebook, in dated sessions.
  • Attempts are recorded quietly as notes you can review and delete, and are never put on a page without Accept.
  • Reading together is its own activity.
  • Each notebook has Goals in your words. Progress is evidence from attempts and pages, never a score, and only you add, reword, or close a goal.
  • One record shape for everything learned, with the author stamped by the app and links to evidence that must exist (how knowledge is kept).
  • Sophia writes notes at the end of a session, not live; your words are kept only if they match the conversation exactly.

  • How long Undo lasts. Here, until your next message. After that, use the previous version or ask for a revision.

What Sophia can and can't write

Sophia can

  • Propose pages and revisions (they wait for you).
  • At the end of a session: note your attempts, read them, raise questions, suggest one next step.
  • When you return: write a briefing that links every claim to its evidence.
  • Suggest a goal in conversation, for you to add if you want it.

The app won't let Sophia

  • Write anything as you, or change your words.
  • Save a page, accept a proposal, or set a goal's status.
  • Keep a note that rests on nothing, or cites something that doesn't exist.
  • Keep a quote that isn't word for word in what you said that session.
  • Record a level, profile, or score of you. A judgment with no evidence behind it can't be stored.
In the command-line experiment there are no screens yet. You add a goal with /goal <text>, correct one of Sophia's records with /correct <record-id> <text>, and print the learning log with each record's id.

Both shrink the key/value side of attention, which cuts the size of the KV cache and the memory bandwidth needed while decoding. They differ in how far they shrink it.

  • Multi-head (the baseline): H query heads, each with its own key and value head.
  • Multi-query: all H query heads share one key/value head. Introduced by Noam Shazeer (2019).
  • Grouped-query: query heads are split into G groups that each share one key/value head. G = H is multi-head and G = 1 is multi-query. Introduced by Ainslie et al. (2023), who also show how to convert an existing multi-head checkpoint with a small amount of extra training.

Per layer, with the same head size, the KV cache scales with the number of key/value heads: H : G : 1.

On quality, the GQA paper reports results close to multi-head at speeds close to multi-query. I'm going on its abstract; I haven't checked the tables in detail, and you declined the blog that summarised them, so nothing here relies on it.

Why the Fiedler vector splits a graph

Accepted Oct 14

✎The quadratic form

For any assignment of values x to the vertices:

x⊤Lx = Σ(i,j)∈E (xi − xj)2

It's small when neighbours get similar values. The constant vector scores zero and carries no information, so we look for the smoothest assignment that isn't constant: minimise x⊤Lx with x ⊥ 1 and ‖x‖ = 1. The minimiser is the second eigenvector of L, the Fiedler vector.

Relaxing the cut

If x ∈ {±1}n, each cut edge contributes 4 and every other edge contributes 0, so x⊤Lx = 4 × edges cut. Requiring x ⊥ 1 pushes toward balanced sides. Dropping the ±1 constraint gives the Fiedler vector as the best real-valued relaxation of this balanced cut (RatioCut), not of the minimum cut. Its signs give a partition.

Worked example: two joined triangles

Vertices 1–3 and 4–6 form triangles, and one edge joins 3 and 4. The Fiedler vector is positive on one triangle and negative on the other. The bridge endpoints sit nearer zero than the outer vertices: shrinking them lowers the cost of the one cut edge more than it raises the cost inside the triangles.

The quadratic form

For any assignment of values x to the vertices, x⊤Lx = Σ(i,j)∈E (xi − xj)2. It's small when neighbours get similar values. The constant vector scores zero and carries no information, so we minimise x⊤Lx with x ⊥ 1 and ‖x‖ = 1. The minimiser is the Fiedler vector.

Relaxing the cut

If x ∈ {±1}n, each cut edge contributes 4 and every other edge contributes 0, so x⊤Lx = 4 × edges cut. Requiring x ⊥ 1 pushes toward balanced sides. Dropping the ±1 constraint gives the Fiedler vector as the best real-valued relaxation of this balanced cut (RatioCut), not of the minimum cut. Its signs give a partition. For ±1 vectors, orthogonality to 1 means exactly equal sides; for real-valued vectors it's only a soft push, since a few large values can offset many small ones. Balance is a soft constraint

For x ∈ {±1}n, Σxi = 0 holds exactly when the two sides have the same number of vertices, so orthogonality to 1 is the balance constraint.

For real-valued x it is weaker. Take four vertices and x = (3, −1, −1, −1): it sums to zero, yet its signs split one vertex against three. A few large values can offset many small ones, so the relaxed problem can favour lopsided sign patterns that the ±1 problem would rule out.

This is one reason the zero threshold can give a poor cut. See the open question below.

Normalized cut and the random-walk Laplacian

Why normalize

RatioCut balances the number of vertices on each side. Ncut balances their total degree instead, so a side made of a few well-connected vertices counts as large. Relaxing Ncut leads to the generalized problem Lx = λDx, which is the eigenproblem of the random-walk Laplacian Lrw = I − D−1A.

Why it matters

On graphs with very uneven degrees the two can split differently. von Luxburg recommends the normalized version, and Lrw in particular.

Sources
U. von Luxburg, "A Tutorial on Spectral Clustering," Statistics and Computing 17 (2007).
