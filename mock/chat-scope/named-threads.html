1.3 The convolution theorem

Written by Sophia Oct 8 · revised Oct 16 · one line is yours

Statement

If y[n] = Σk x[k] h[n − k], then Y(ω) = X(ω) H(ω). Convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency. The dual also holds: multiplying two sequences in time convolves their spectra in frequency, scaled by 1/2π.

Derivation for the DTFT

Write Y(ω) = Σn y[n] e−iωn and substitute the convolution sum. Swapping the order of summation leaves Σk x[k] Σn h[n − k] e−iωn. With m = n − k, the inner sum is e−iωk H(ω), and what remains of the outer sum is X(ω). The swap is justified when x and h are absolutely summable.

In your words · Oct 9
"Swap the sums, substitute m = n − k, and the inner sum is just X(ω) times a phase factor."

What it buys you: filters

A filter's output is its input convolved with the impulse response, so in frequency every component of the input is scaled by H(ω). That turns "what does this filter do?" into reading one function. A moving-average filter is a low-pass filter because its frequency response is a sinc. Averaging L samples passes slow variation and attenuates fast oscillation.

Where it fails

The theorem is about linear convolution of sequences whose DTFTs exist. The DFT samples the spectrum at N points, and multiplying two N-point DFTs gives circular convolution, which wraps the tail of the result back onto its start.

Circular convolution of length-N sequences equals linear convolution only when N ≥ L₁ + L₂ − 1. your line · Oct 16

Zero-padding both sequences to that length before the DFT makes the product match the linear result.

Source
A. V. Oppenheim and R. W. Schafer, Discrete-Time Signal Processing, 3rd ed., §2.9, "Properties of the discrete-time Fourier transform."
Location: §2.9.6, "The convolution theorem"

1.4 Sampling and aliasing

Written by Sophia Oct 10

Sampling as multiplication by an impulse train

Sampling x(t) every T seconds can be modelled as multiplying it by an impulse train, p(t) = Σn δ(t − nT). The product keeps the values x(nT) and discards everything between them.

Replication in frequency

The spectrum of the sampled signal is X(ω) repeated at every multiple of the sampling frequency ωs = 2π/T, scaled by 1/T. Each copy is identical; only its position differs.

The Nyquist condition

If x(t) has no energy above ωmax and ωs > 2ωmax, the copies don't overlap, and x(t) can be recovered with a low-pass filter. If they overlap, high frequencies fold onto low ones. After sampling, a folded component is indistinguishable from genuine low-frequency content: that is aliasing.

Source
A. V. Oppenheim and A. S. Willsky, Signals and Systems, 2nd ed., §7.1, "Representation of a continuous-time signal by its samples."
Location: §7.1.1, "Impulse-train sampling"

1.4 Sampling and aliasing

Written by Sophia Oct 10

Sampling as multiplication by an impulse train

Sampling x(t) every T seconds can be modelled as multiplying it by an impulse train, p(t) = Σn δ(t − nT). The product keeps the values x(nT) and discards everything between them.

Replication in frequency

The spectrum of the sampled signal is X(ω) repeated at every multiple of the sampling frequency ωs = 2π/T, scaled by 1/T. Each copy is identical; only its position differs.

The Nyquist condition

If x(t) has no energy above ωmax and ωs > 2ωmax, the copies don't overlap, and x(t) can be recovered with a low-pass filter. If they overlap, high frequencies fold onto low ones. After sampling, a folded component is indistinguishable from genuine low-frequency content: that is aliasing.

Source
A. V. Oppenheim and A. S. Willsky, Signals and Systems, 2nd ed., §7.1, "Representation of a continuous-time signal by its samples."

0.3 Orthogonality and why the coefficients fall out

Written by Sophia Oct 3, alongside 0.2

Inner products of exponentials

Over one period T, the integral of eikω₀t · e−imω₀t is T when k = m and 0 otherwise. The exponentials behave like perpendicular axes.

Reading off a coefficient

Multiply the series by e−imω₀t and integrate over a period. Every term except the m-th integrates to zero, so cm = (1/T) ∫ x(t) e−imω₀t dt. That is why the coefficient formula needs no system of equations.

2.2 Zero-padding and interpolation

Written by Sophia Oct 6 · revised Oct 14

What padding does

Appending zeros to an N-sample signal before a longer DFT samples the same DTFT at more, closer-spaced frequencies. The plotted spectrum looks smoother because you are seeing more points of one curve.

What it doesn't do

Padding adds no information about the signal. Two peaks closer than about 2π/N stay merged however many zeros you add; only a longer record separates them.

0.3 added to this thread
Sophia

Because over one period each cross term is ei(k−m)ω₀t, which goes round the unit circle a whole number of times and integrates to zero.

Only k = m leaves a constant, and that integrates to T.
